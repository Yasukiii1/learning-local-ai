The Water Cycle

The water cycle is the journey water takes as it moves around the Earth. The same water has been moving around for millions of years. The Sun gives the energy that keeps the water cycle going.

Evaporation

When the Sun heats water in oceans, lakes and rivers, the water turns into an invisible gas called water vapour. This is called evaporation. Warm water evaporates faster than cold water. Plants also give off water vapour from their leaves, and this is called transpiration.

Condensation

Water vapour rises into the sky, where the air is colder. The cold air makes the vapour turn back into tiny drops of liquid water. This is called condensation. Millions of these tiny drops together make a cloud. You can see condensation at home when a cold glass gets wet on the outside.

Precipitation

When the drops in a cloud join together, they get bigger and heavier. When they are too heavy to float, they fall to the ground. Water falling from clouds is called precipitation. It can be rain, snow, sleet or hail. Snow forms when the air is below 0 degrees Celsius.

Collection

When precipitation reaches the ground, it collects in oceans, lakes and rivers. Some water soaks into the soil and becomes groundwater. Groundwater can stay underground for a very long time. Water that flows over the land into rivers is called runoff. Then the Sun heats the water again, and the cycle starts all over.

Why the water cycle matters

The water cycle brings fresh water to people, animals and plants. Without it, there would be no rain to fill rivers or to help crops grow. About 97 percent of the water on Earth is salty ocean water, so the fresh water brought by rain is very important.
